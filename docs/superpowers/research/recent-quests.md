# OSRS Dialogue Research: Recent Quests (2018-2025, outside Varlamore)

Research slice covering the "modern Jagex house style" of NPC dialogue writing, drawn from OSRS wiki quest transcripts for quests released 2018-2025, excluding Varlamore content. All quotes are verbatim (fair-use length, under 125 characters) and attributed to speaker and quest.

## 2. Sentence and line shape

- **Short lines dominate.** Most individual dialogue bubbles run 5-20 words. Long expository paragraphs are rare and are usually reserved for one "lore dump" NPC per quest (Wizard Persten in *Temple of the Eye*, Regath in *Client of Kourend*, the Archmage's warnings).
- **Declarative-then-clarify rhythm.** NPCs often state a short flat fact, then a second short sentence that colours or complicates it: *"Alas, I've hit a bit of a blocker."* (Veos, X Marks the Spot) followed by a plain ask for help.
- **Fragments and ellipses used for hesitation or menace**, especially in horror/mystery beats: *"This could be my chance to take advantage of the situation..."* (Player narration, Misthalin Mystery).
- **Formal-register NPCs (councillors, archmages, kings) use longer, subordinate-clause sentences**; working-class or comic NPCs (farmers, cooks, sailors) use short, clipped ones. This register gap is a deliberate characterisation tool, not inconsistency.
- **Broken/accented speech is used sparingly and only for specific non-human or dialect groups** (goblins, giants, Corsair Cove sailors), never for generic humans — see section 5.
- **Narration boxes are short and neutral**, almost always present tense, third person about the player: *"You dig up an Ancient Casket. As you do, you hear a faint whispering."* (X Marks the Spot).

## 3. Humour catalogue

### a. Deadpan player narration (self-aware asides)
- "Sucker." — Player narration, after tricking Tomas Lawry, *A Kingdom Divided*
- "Weird... What sort of monster charges at someone and then steals a bunch of carrots?" — Player, *A Porcine of Interest*
- "It somehow looks even worse than before!" — Player narration, *Getting Ahead*
- "Gosh, that was horrible and so drawn out!" — Player, *Misthalin Mystery*
**Rule:** the player's inner voice gets one dry, slightly self-deprecating aside per beat, usually undercutting the player's own effort or the situation's absurdity — not a joke told *at* another character.

### b. NPC understatement / dry deflation
- "I'm being thorough." — Martin Holt, deflecting an accusation of laziness, *A Kingdom Divided*
- "Well, good job I found it again." — Wizard Persten, on losing an ancient artefact, *Temple of the Eye*
- "Well... that's probably not true, but let's pretend otherwise." — Wizard Persten, *Desert Treasure II*
- "Shocking!" — Dr Banikan, after a failed golem attempt, *Desert Treasure II*
**Rule:** competent-but-flawed NPCs comment on their own failures in the flattest possible tone; the joke is the gap between the stakes and the delivery.

### c. Absurdist/meta humour (rare, used as a treat, not a habit)
- "Old School RuneScape isn't that sort of fantasy game, in case ye hadn't been told that a-fore today." — Ithoi the Navigator, *The Corsair Curse*
- "Any sufficiently advanced technology be indistinguishable from demonic possession, that it be." — Captain Tock, *The Corsair Curse*
- "We're not playing Name Your Favourite Body Part." — Gnocci the Cook, *The Corsair Curse*
**Rule:** one quest per cluster gets a genuinely wink-at-the-camera line, almost always from a comic-relief secondary character, never from a quest-critical authority figure.

### d. Character-driven bickering / needling
- "The only thing without brains is you!" — Gordon, *Getting Ahead*
- "Do you want happy rhyming Leenz, or angry, gruff and pain-inflicting Leenz?" — Leenz, *Client of Kourend*
- "You don't look much like a Tim/Sue/Sam." — Knight of Ardougne, *Song of the Elves*
**Rule:** humour comes from a character's established quirk (a rhyming scheme, an obsession, a temperament) being poked at, rather than a generic pun or wordplay.

### e. How this differs from the 2004-era style
Older (2001-2010) OSRS quest writing leaned heavily on outright puns, silly names, and slapstick non-sequiturs delivered by almost any NPC equally. The 2018-2025 sample here shows a clear shift:
- Humour is now **attached to specific, recurring personalities** (Veos, Ithoi, Leenz, Gordon) rather than being a generic tone applied to every NPC.
- **Puns are rare**; wordplay largely disappears in favour of situational irony and character consistency.
- **Player-narration asides carry more of the comic load** than in older quests, giving the player character a drier, more modern "sitcom protagonist" voice.
- Comedy is more often **undercut by real stakes nearby** (Corsair Curse's murder plot sits one line away from its jokes; Misthalin Mystery is dark comedy inside a horror plot) — tonal whiplash is intentional, not accidental, in modern writing.

## 4. Refusal and deflection patterns

- **Bureaucratic refusal via "process," not malice:** "The people can express their concerns through proper channels." (Tomas Lawry, A Kingdom Divided); "That's not our problem. We're just here to follow orders." (Sergeant, Getting Ahead); "The council serves the king I'm afraid, we can't overrule his decisions." (Councillor Halgrive, Song of the Elves).
- **Flat "no" followed by a one-line justification**, rarely apologetic beyond a token "I'm afraid": "No. I can't take any risks. Now I'm afraid I have business to attend to." (King Lathas, Song of the Elves).
- **Suspicion-based refusal, demanding proof or reason:** "Why should I be so trusting to you?" (Regath, Client of Kourend); "I don't have any reason to trust that." (Kasonde, Desert Treasure II).
- **Irritated dismissal of interruption**, common for scholars/mages absorbed in work: "Quiet! You must not break my concentration!" (Dark Mage, Temple of the Eye); "What? Are you trying to waste my time?" (Dark Mage, Temple of the Eye).
- **Deflection by redirecting to someone else or "come back later":** "I don't know how to solve it I'm afraid. Maybe look for someone named Bob?" (Veos, X Marks the Spot).
- **Refusal escalating to threat/venom from antagonists:** "This game didn't involve you! Now her death is on you!" (Killer, Misthalin Mystery); "Then may your death come quick, human." (Catalytic Guardian, Desert Treasure II).
**Pattern rule:** refusal is almost never a bare "no" — it is a no plus a stated (even flimsy) reason, so the NPC reads as having agency and logic rather than being a gate that blocks progress arbitrarily.

## 5. How NPCs address and treat the player, and how authority changes tone

- Ordinary townsfolk and clients speak to the player as a **known, competent equal or hired hand** — direct requests, first names, minimal ceremony: "Anything you need from me?" / "Need a hand with anything?" (Getting Ahead).
- **Authority figures (kings, archmages, commanders, council members) use formal, slightly clipped diction** and often remind the player of their subordinate status even while asking for help: "You are only apprentices. You should go back to your studies." (Archmage Sedridor, Temple of the Eye); "Let it be known that I consider this a bad idea." (Sedridor).
- **Guards and low-level officials are brusque, rule-bound, and largely indifferent to the player's stakes**: "That's not our problem. We're just here to follow orders." (Sergeant, Getting Ahead); "Sorry citizen. Only Knights of Ardougne are allowed in the prison." (Knight of Ardougne, Song of the Elves).
- **Non-human or dialect-marked NPCs (goblins, giants, cave dwellers) get simplified or broken grammar as a marker of otherness**, but are still written with clear internal logic and even humour, not just as a joke register: "Me want get into temple?" / "That knowledge not for ordinary goblin." (Land of the Goblins); Kovac's fragmented, ellipsis-heavy speech in Sleeping Giants that visibly *improves* over the quest as a character-growth device.
- **Antagonists/mysterious figures speak in short, control-asserting sentences** and rarely explain themselves fully until a late reveal: "We're going to play a game. Do you like to play games?" (Killer, Misthalin Mystery); "Knowledge can be a curse. Sometimes, it is better to remain unaware." (Veos' Client, Client of Kourend).
- Tone shift with authority is consistent: **the higher the NPC's institutional power, the more clipped/formal and the less willing to over-explain**; **the lower the NPC's power (cabin boys, cooks, farmhands), the more chatty, digressive, and willing to joke.**

## 6. Player-line conventions

- Player options are almost always **short, first-person, and end-goal-focused**: "What am I meant to be doing?" / "I'd better get going." / "I'll be back."
- The player rarely monologues; when longer player lines occur they are usually **restating a plan back to an NPC for confirmation**: "Okay, I'll help, but first you need to tell me what this place was used for?" (Sleeping Giants).
- **Player narration boxes (unspoken thoughts) carry sarcasm and internal commentary** that spoken dialogue options mostly avoid: "Would you look at that... Apparently our monster is picky." (A Porcine of Interest).
- Player options frequently include a **polite refusal/stalling choice** alongside the "progress" choice: "I don't need to go back down there right now." / "Not yet." / "I'm good thanks." — giving players a non-committal exit without punishing them narratively.
- Player voice stays **even-keeled and professional as an investigative or helpful anchor**, even in dramatic quests — emotional reactions are rationed and land harder because they're rare: "Leave her alone you monster!" (Misthalin Mystery).

## 7. Option-menu conventions

Representative verbatim menu sets:
- "What's all this about?" / "So you already have some leads?" / "Did you know there's a demon upstairs?" (A Kingdom Divided)
- "Where do you get your Bloody Bracers from?" / "I'd better get going." (Sins of the Father)
- "What did the monster look like?" / "Why are you helping me?" / "I think that'll be all for now." (A Porcine of Interest)
- "Can I help?" / "I'm good thanks." / "If you say so..." (X Marks the Spot)
- "Who are you?" / "Let's do it." / "Not yet." (Temple of the Eye)
- "Have you got any quests for me?" / "What am I meant to be doing, again?" (Client of Kourend)

Conventions observed:
- Menus mix **information-seeking questions, a commit/progress choice, and a stalling/exit choice.**
- A generic **"What am I meant to be doing?"**-style catch-up option recurs across many quests, letting a player who forgot the plot re-orient without breaking immersion.
- Menus are typically **4-8 options**, phrased as complete short sentences or clauses, never single words except for pure yes/no confirmations ("Yes." / "No.").
- **Sign-off options are near-universal** ("I'll be back." / "That'll be all for now." / "See you around.") giving every conversation a clean exit line.

## 8. Narration box conventions

- Always **third person, present tense, describing the player's action or the scene**: "You rummage through the box... You find a bunch of metal bars and old weapons!" (A Porcine of Interest).
- Used for **cutscene stage direction** in a terse, almost script-like register: "A cutscene begins. The player and Commander Fullore approach Kingstown." (A Kingdom Divided); "Ithoi rushes out of the hut." (The Corsair Curse).
- Used to **deliver consequence or system feedback plainly**, without embellishment: "Sarah hands you a heavy pouch of 5,000 coins." (A Porcine of Interest); "The orb shatters after being held in the air." (Client of Kourend).
- Can carry **dark or unsettling beats in a flat, unemphatic register**, letting content rather than style create horror: "You failed to keep Kasonde alive" (Desert Treasure II); "As you jot down the final information on Arceuus, you hear a mysterious whisper in your ear." (Client of Kourend).
- Rarely editorialises — narration states what happens, and any wit is reserved for player-thought asides embedded as quasi-narration (see section 6), not the impartial descriptive boxes.

## 9. Sea, ship, dock and guard flavour

**The Corsair Curse (pirates, sailors, captain):**
- Heavy dialect: "arrr," "ye/yer," dropped g's, "a-fore," "be" for "is": "Arrr! Any sufficiently advanced technology be indistinguishable from demonic possession, that it be." (Captain Tock)
- Nautical idiom used for authority/duty statements: "A captain shouldn't leave his ship in a time of crisis. Rule of the sea, that is." (Captain Tock)
- Old-salt bravado and repetition-for-emphasis: "I sailed the seas as a man, like I sailed 'em as a boy." (Ithoi the Navigator)
- Blessing/toast-style farewell speech: "Ye be brave in the heart, strong in the arm and full in the bowels, an' I be grateful to ye." (Captain Tock)
- Suspicion runs hot but is voiced with a shrug rather than menace: "Bah! Ye can't even think of a reason why I'd want to poison the crew..." (Ithoi the Navigator)

**Sins of the Father (Port/Darkmeyer-adjacent, cabin boy, tavern):**
- Tavern-keeper terseness about goods: "We serve Crombwick's finest Bloody Bracer, that is all." (Carl)
- Merchant deflection about supply chains: "My supplier is a private individual. He doesn't like others meddling in his affairs." (Carl)
- Cabin-boy nervousness, understated: "I hope he won't be long. I'm not really cut out for this." (Cabin Boy Herbert)

**A Kingdom Divided (Piscarilius officials/dock bureaucracy):**
- Guard/official evasiveness about a missing person, phrased as plausible local gossip: "Well, if she did it wasn't recently. We'd have noticed a councillor." (Sergeant, Farming Guild)
- Domestic-staff deflection with mild loyalty: "Not as far as I can recall. Councillor Hughes used to order things." (Horace)

**Sleeping Giants (Giants' Foundry, blacksmith trade talk):**
- Trade instruction delivered in short, almost manual-style clauses: "Trip hammer power by water... Water spin wheel and raise hammer." (Kovac)
- Craft pride phrased plainly: "This looks like it'll do the trick." (Player, assessing forged sword)
- Warning delivered casually rather than urgently: "Just no swim in the hot juice." (Kovac, re: lava)

**Cross-cutting pattern:** dock/ship/trade NPCs get their register from *trade jargon and duty/rule-of-the-sea framing*, not just accent — a sailor cites "rule of the sea," a blacksmith cites tool names and process steps, a merchant cites supplier confidentiality. Accent (dropped consonants, "arrr," "ye") is reserved almost exclusively for pirates/old sailors, not applied uniformly to every dock worker or guard.

## 10. Ten "do" rules and ten "don't" rules

**Do:**
1. Give refusals a stated (even weak) reason — "no, because X" beats a bare "no."
2. Let institutional power correlate with clipped, formal diction; let low-status NPCs be chattier and warmer.
3. Reserve accent/dialect for a specific, narratively marked group (pirates, goblins, giants) — not as a generic "flavour" layer.
4. Put dry wit in the player's own narration/thought asides, not just in NPC mouths.
5. Give every conversation a clean sign-off line as a menu option ("I'll be back," "That'll be all for now").
6. Keep narration boxes flat, third-person, present-tense, and unembellished — let content, not style, carry weight or horror.
7. Attach humour to an established character trait (a rhyme scheme, an obsession, a temperament) rather than a one-off pun.
8. Vary sentence length by role: short clipped lines for guards/action beats, longer subordinate-clause lines for scholars/nobles.
9. Include a stalling/non-committal player option alongside the "progress" option so players aren't railroaded.
10. Let comic and dark tones sit close together within the same quest — tonal contrast, used deliberately, reads as modern and confident.

**Don't:**
1. Don't make every NPC equally witty — comedy should cluster around specific comic-relief characters.
2. Don't use puns as a default humour tool — modern OSRS writing has largely moved away from wordplay gags.
3. Don't give generic dock workers or guards pirate-style accents just because they're near water — reserve dialect markers.
4. Don't let authority figures over-explain themselves; the more powerful the NPC, the less they need to justify a decision.
5. Don't write bare "no" refusals with no accompanying reason — it reads as a game-logic wall, not a character choice.
6. Don't editorialise inside narration boxes — save wit and emotion for dialogue and player-thought asides.
7. Don't make broken/accented speech mock the character — write it with internal logic and even give it moments of humour or growth (see Kovac).
8. Don't let every menu have only "yes, progress" options — always leave an information or exit option.
9. Don't drop dialect/accent markers inconsistently — if a character says "ye" once, keep using it, not standard English elsewhere in the same quest.
10. Don't pad every exchange with long sentences; most functional NPC lines should be short enough to read at a glance (roughly 5-20 words).
